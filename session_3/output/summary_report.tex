\title{The Swiss Franc Shock and Polish Voter Attitudes:\\
Does Information Increase Support for Government Intervention?}
\author{Session 3 Analysis Report}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
In January 2015 the Swiss National Bank (SNB) unexpectedly abandoned its
currency floor, sending the Swiss franc sharply higher against the Polish zloty
and raising monthly payments for the many Polish households holding
franc-denominated mortgages. Using a nationally representative survey of 2{,}044
Polish adults fielded in October 2015 with an embedded randomized information
treatment, we ask whether providing information about the crisis increases
support for government intervention to help affected borrowers. We merge the
survey with administrative demographics, estimate weighted logistic regressions
of intervention support on the randomized treatments, and subject the baseline
estimate to a battery of robustness checks. Factual information about the franc
shock raises support for intervention by a statistically significant margin
(coefficient $0.302$, $p=0.016$), while historical-context and Hungary-comparison
framings do not. Direct exposure to a current foreign-currency (FX) loan is a far
stronger predictor of support than any framing. The information effect is robust
to weighting and to the addition of demographic controls on the full sample, but
loses conventional significance once income and ideology controls shrink the
analytic sample by roughly 44\% --- a missing-data artifact rather than
confounding. We conclude that information shifts attitudes on the margin, but
that personal economic exposure dominates, and that sample composition, not the
controls themselves, drives the apparent fragility of the effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The 15 January 2015 removal of the SNB's CHF/EUR floor was a textbook external
economic shock: unanticipated, large, and unevenly distributed across the
population. Figure~\ref{fig:fx} shows the resulting discontinuity in the
CHF/PLN exchange rate, which fed directly into the monthly payments of Polish
households with franc-denominated mortgages. This report studies the political
consequences of that shock using survey data described in the course codebook,
drawn from Ahlquist, Copelovitch, and Walter, ``The Political Consequences of
External Economic Shocks,'' \textit{American Journal of Political Science}
(2020).

The research question is whether \emph{providing information} about the crisis
increases support for government intervention to help affected borrowers, and
whether a respondent's own FX-loan exposure moderates that effect. Because the
information framing was randomly assigned, the treatment contrasts have a causal
interpretation within the surveyed population.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Data and linkage}

The analysis draws on two files. The survey itself,
\texttt{swiss\_franc\_survey.csv}, records responses from 2{,}044 Polish adults
interviewed between 7 and 21 October 2015, and contains the randomized
information treatment, the intervention-support outcome, each respondent's
FX-loan status, a standard set of demographics, and survey weights. A companion
file, \texttt{respondent\_demographics.csv}, adds two attitudinal and
socioeconomic measures collected separately: household income quintile and
left--right ideological self-placement.

Linking the two requires care. The demographics file holds 2{,}994 records, of
which only 1{,}994 correspond to actual survey respondents; the remaining
thousand are identifiers that match no one, and a further 50 genuine respondents
never appear in it at all. We therefore retain the survey as the master frame and
attach demographics through a left join, which yields exactly the intended
2{,}044 rows, leaves demographics missing for the 50 unmatched respondents, and
silently drops the spurious identifiers. The alternative join types each
compromise the sample, either by discarding genuine respondents or by importing
phantom ones. To confirm the linkage behaved as intended, we checked the final
row count, verified that respondent identifiers remained unique, and reproduced
the expected treatment-arm sizes (480 control, 549 information, 520 history, and
495 Hungary).

\subsection{Measures and analytic sample}

The dependent variable, \texttt{supports\_intervention}, takes the value one for
respondents who favor either ``some'' or ``big'' government intervention and zero
otherwise; the 8 respondents who answered ``don't know'' or left the item blank
are set aside, leaving 2{,}036 with a usable outcome. The key independent
variable is the randomly assigned framing, which took one of four forms ---
control, factual information, historical context, or a comparison with Hungary's
policy response --- with the control condition serving as the reference category
throughout. We distinguish three levels of personal exposure to the shock:
respondents currently repaying an FX loan (69), those who held one in the past
(55), and those who never did (1{,}918). Consistent with the codebook, an income
quintile of zero denotes ``no income'' and is retained as a substantive category
rather than coded as missing. Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarizes the merged
sample.

Income and ideology are missing for roughly a quarter of records by construction
(Table~\ref{tab:demosummary} and the appendix). Because these two controls are
the only ones with appreciable missingness, they govern how the analytic sample
contracts as the specification grows --- a point that proves decisive for
interpreting the results.

\subsection{Estimation strategy}

We model the binary outcome with weighted logistic regression. Writing
$p_i = \Pr(\texttt{supports\_intervention}_i = 1)$, the baseline specification is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit}(p_i)
  = \beta_0
  + \beta_1 \,\text{Info}_i
  + \beta_2 \,\text{History}_i
  + \beta_3 \,\text{Hungary}_i ,
\label{eq:base}
\end{equation}
where each treatment indicator is measured against the omitted control group, so
the coefficients recover the causal effect of each framing relative to receiving
no information. We then build outward from Equation~\eqref{eq:base} in stages,
adding FX-exposure indicators, the core demographic controls (age, gender,
education, and urban--rural settlement), and finally the income and ideology
measures. Because the model is fit by listwise deletion, we record the analytic
$N$ at every stage so that any change in the estimates can be attributed to the
covariates or to the shrinking sample rather than conflated between the two. The
robustness battery then perturbs the baseline along four dimensions: the presence
of survey weights, the breadth of the control set, the treatment
parameterization (the three arms separately versus a single ``any information''
indicator), and the estimation sample (all respondents versus urban dwellers
only).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Main estimates}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the baseline and controls-augmented weighted
logistic regressions. The factual information treatment raises support for
intervention relative to the control group ($0.302$, $p=0.016$ in the baseline),
and the estimate is stable when FX exposure and demographic controls are added on
the full sample ($0.324$). Neither the historical-context nor the Hungary
treatment produces a statistically significant effect. By far the largest
coefficient belongs to \emph{current} FX exposure ($1.287$, $p<0.01$):
respondents personally repaying a franc loan are dramatically more supportive of
intervention, independent of any framing they received.

\subsection{Robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:robustness} arrays six specifications side by side; the compiled
version is reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:robustness}. The information effect
survives dropping the survey weights (column 2, $0.290$, $p=0.021$) and adding
demographic controls on the full sample (column 3, $0.324$, $p=0.011$). Pooling
the three framings into a single ``any information'' dummy yields a smaller but
still significant effect (column 5, $0.218$, $p=0.042$), and restricting to urban
respondents leaves the estimate essentially unchanged though borderline (column
6, $0.326$, $p=0.051$).

The one specification in which the effect loses significance is column 4, which
adds the income and ideology controls. Here the coefficient remains positive and
comparable in magnitude ($0.267$) but $p$ rises to $0.118$. The reason is sample
composition rather than confounding: listwise deletion of missing income and
ideology drops the analytic sample from 2{,}035 to 1{,}151 --- about 44\% --- and
the loss of precision, not a change in the point estimate, drives the result.
Across every specification, current FX exposure remains large and highly
significant.

\section{Conclusion}

Factual information about the Swiss franc shock measurably increased Polish
respondents' support for government intervention to help affected borrowers,
whereas historical and comparative framings did not. The effect is modest but
robust to weighting, to control specification on the full sample, and to a pooled
treatment definition. Its apparent fragility under the fullest control set is a
missing-data artifact --- a 44\% sample reduction from listwise deletion of
income and ideology --- rather than evidence that the controls explain the effect
away. The most substantively important finding is that personal economic
exposure dominates information: respondents currently repaying a franc loan were
far more supportive of intervention than any framing could induce. For applied
work, the analysis is a reminder to predict and report how the analytic sample
changes as controls are added, and to distinguish a vanishing point estimate
from a vanishing standard error.
